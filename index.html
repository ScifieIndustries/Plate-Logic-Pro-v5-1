Plate Logic

A finishing check for anyone who cooks well but wants their plate to look like it too. It runs on one rule, taken from years of plating dishes to order: figure out what's heavy, add something to balance it, then check the color reads right.

1. Heavy

Name what's doing the rich, fatty, or dense work on the plate — the sauce, the cooking method, the protein itself.

2. Balance

If the richness isn't matched by acid, something sharp needs to cut through it — not more richness.

3. Contrast

Check the color last. If the plate reads as one dominant color, add a garnish that visually pops against it.

In practice: a seared steak with béarnaise is already rich and already acidic — the béarnaise brings its own fat and tang — so the fix is a fresh, light color: shaved fennel over more green beans. A dish like Stjerneskud/Shootingstar, built on a rich pink dressing, needs both acid (lemon) and a deliberate green element — asparagus, cucumber, pea shoots — because the whole plate reads red otherwise.

Why plates fall flat

It tastes right, looks flat

The dish is cooked well but reads as one dull color and one texture — nothing tells the eye "this was considered."

Guessing at acid

Hard to tell if a dish already has enough sharpness from the sauce, or if it actually needs something added to cut through it.

No feel for the right garnish

Herbs on everything isn't a system — the right garnish depends on what's already rich, acidic, and colored on that specific plate.

Two real plates, worked through

Seared steak, béarnaise. The béarnaise already brings fat and acid, so more richness or more sharpness both miss the point. What's missing is color and freshness — shaved fennel instead of another pile of green beans, so the plate doesn't just taste balanced, it looks lifted.
Stjerneskud/Shootingstar. Built on a rich pink dressing, toasted bread, fish and shrimp — everything on this plate reads red and heavy. It needs acid (lemon) it doesn't have yet, and a deliberate green element — asparagus, cucumber, pea shoots — or the whole thing looks like one color from across the room.
